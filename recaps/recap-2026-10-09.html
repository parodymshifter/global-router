Global Router Recap

Recap 4 ended with the router matching the ISPD 2024 contest on score but taking 16.9 hours on the largest design it could fit, against the winner's 3.6 minutes. Since then that run has come down to 511 s: 119 times faster, with a slightly better score. Most of the gain came from removing work the 2024 score does not reward, the rest from threads that give the same answer at any thread count. The router now beats the contest's best raw score on all six hidden designs and the best beta submission on all seven public ones, and on the four smallest hidden designs it is also faster than the contest winner.

Since 8 October

  1. Part 115, re-ablating for the 2024 score. The pipeline was built for ISPD 2008, where only overflow and wire count. Re-tested stage by stage under the 2024 score: wirelength recovery made the score worse, layer assignment's later rounds gained ±0.01%, and the region, refinement and fallback stages changed nothing. All dropped (fast24.env). Then pd36route removed redundant work inside the remaining stages, byte-identical: incremental cost refresh in the pattern stage, per-piece prefix sums, cached via terms. 61,017 s → 1,599 s. Every run now ends with a per-stage report: time, footprint, score and its change, work done.
  2. Part 116, threads. pd37route runs layer assignment and the 3D passes on a dependency graph, and the pattern stage's solve in parallel: a net waits only for earlier nets whose regions overlap its own, so every net sees exactly the state it would see sequentially. Byte-identical at 1, 4 and 8 threads, with no per-thread copies. 1,599 s → 623 s.
  3. Part 117, I/O and memory. Faster readers and a threaded writer, per-thread scratch, data freed as soon as stages finish with it. 553 s, peak 22.6 GB, still byte-identical.
  4. Part 118, the slow tier on threads. The exact dependency graph could not help here: a trace showed the hard nets' search boxes pile onto the same hotspots (54,283 nets, longest chain 19,176), capping it at 2.8× and in practice 1×. The 2008 router's batched scheme replaced it: batches of 64 searched in parallel against a snapshot, committed in net order, conflicts carried to the next batch. Same routes at any thread count, a better score on all 13 designs (−0.0005% to −0.06%), and the slow tier faster on both cluster designs (mempool_cluster 392 s → 268 s, mempool_cluster_ranking 75 s → 41 s). Dropping the third 3D pass was also tested: 6–8% less time for a 0.01–0.04% worse score, so it stays.
  5. Part 119, batches spread over the chip. Expansion counts showed the batched slow tier threw away 78% of its search work: consecutive nets sit together, so each batch of 64 landed on one hotspot, every member rerouted at once, and most collided. Dealing each round's nets round-robin over 64 × 64-GCell tiles cut first-round collisions from 20,740 to 2,784. That made batches of 256 pay off, and mempool_cluster's slow tier fell from 268 s to 137 s (809 s → 658 s overall). Scores moved within ±0.02%.

Most of it was work, not hardware

Re-ablating under the 2024 score removed 92% of the time with no score loss. Exact cuts inside the remaining stages took another 3×. Threads, the obvious lever, gave the last 3×.

Exact threads where regions commute

Nets whose regions don't overlap give the same result in either order. Scheduling on that fact made threads byte-identical, which kept every earlier comparison valid. Where hard nets pile onto one hotspot, it stops working, and batching takes over.

Where the rest goes

At 8 threads on the hidden cluster: layer assignment's first round 31%, three 3D passes 30%, the pattern stage 25%, the slow tier 7%, read and write 7%. No single stage dominates any more.

Caveats

  • Hardware. Our runs use 8 threads of one laptop-class M4 Pro; the contest machine had 8 cores and 4 A100 GPUs. Neither side's numbers transfer to the other's machine exactly.
  • The cluster designs are still slower than the winner: 511 s against 217 s on the hidden cluster (the contest's #2 took 124 s, #3 275 s), and mempool_group_rank 69 s against 46 s.
  • Margins. The score leads over the contest are 0.46–1.43% on the hidden designs: real, but small.
  • Tuning. All settings come from one design and the gap analysis; nothing is tuned per design.

Room to improve

The full analysis is on Global Router Speed and Theory; the short version:

Time

The hidden cluster takes 511 s against the winner's 217 s. The threads give 1.7× in the pattern stage, 2.5× in the 3D passes and 4.0× in layer assignment, out of a possible 8×. Raising the first two to 4× would cut the public cluster from 658 s to about 480 s (estimate). The rest needs a GPU path, a FLUTE-style tree table for small nets, and a slow tier that doesn't wait on each batch's slowest search. The ISPD 2008 standard is still single-threaded at 1–27 hours on the large benchmarks, and Part 119's fix is a reason to retry threading there.

Wirelength

On the hidden cluster our wire cost is 0.45% above the winner's, while vias are 3.9% and overflow cost 1.5% below. Multi-pin trees are within 0.2–0.8% of a 1-Steiner reference, so better trees can recover up to about half a percent. The wire/via balance in layer assignment is worth one sweep. On ISPD 2008 we are within −0.05% to +2.3% of NTHU-Route 2.0 where overflow is zero, and the 3D pass (−0.13% to −0.45% on all sixteen) awaits one confirmation run.

Overflow

234M of the hidden cluster's 309M overflow cost is the idle-GCell floor no router can avoid, and GCells actually over capacity contribute only 3.3M. The other 71M is busy but legal GCells: room for spreading load (Z shapes and wider detours in the 3D pass). A lower bound per design would size it. On ISPD 2008, bigblue4 (356 vs a best published 166) and newblue7 (108; at least 54 forced, best 68) have the clearest measured room.
