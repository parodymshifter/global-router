Global Router Recap

Five days on one question: where the late stages of an ISPD 2008 run spend their time, and what the overflow they leave behind is doing. The searches turn out to flood their boxes, so late work is box area; the residue on the hard benchmarks is nets trading places in one hotspot, which is exploration, not waste; and deliberately tightened copies of easy designs fall into three distinct regimes. Two exact time savers came out of it (one now in the 2008 standard), a speed setting with two more, eight ideas measured and set aside, and a new safeguard after a bug.

Where the late stages spend their time

The region stage is 70–88% of a run on the designs that keep some overflow, and 94–97% of its search work comes after round 50, when each box reaches 110–200 tiles past its net on grids only 400–490 tiles wide. A trace of every search (Part 138) showed why the boxes cost so much:

Every cell of the box is cheaper to reach than the route the search finally takes, because that route must cross a costly congested wall the heuristic cannot see. So late work is box area, and the levers on the heuristic or on how boxes grow buy little:

Splitting the region stage into independent hotspots on separate threads was ruled out from the same traces: with boxes that wide, no two hotspots on these grids are far enough apart to share no edges.

What the residue is doing

A per-round dump of every overflowed edge (Part 140) on newblue1 and newblue4, all four portfolio variants each:

It oscillates; nothing is stuck

59–88% of the remaining excess sits on edges that go over and under capacity every 2–4 rounds — nets trading places — and no edge stays over for 90% of any 50-round window. On both designs it is a single hotspot: keeping each hotspot's best round separately gains nothing.

Damping it is harmful

A discount for staying on the current route made newblue1 176–220 (from 32) and newblue4 180–226 (from 144): the oscillation is the negotiation exploring, and slowing it is a soft form of the revert rule Part 59 already rejected. A one-round cooldown kept overflow within about 8 units for 30–40% less work.

Annealing refinement does not help

Accepting worse reroutes with falling probability left newblue1 at 30 (T0 0.3 and 1: nothing accepted) and made it 34 at T0 5; newblue4 150 vs 154 once, adaptec1 0.95 −0.6%. Inside the noise.

Nothing early predicts the winning variant

The first 50 rounds' oscillation share ranked newblue1's four variants exactly, but not newblue4's (Spearman −0.2), and it does not predict the next window's gain. The portfolio, which tries several and keeps the best, stays the right tool.

Hard cases from easy parents

To get more related examples of difficult designs, the capacities of five zero-overflow designs were cut (Part 142): every edge to 95%, 90% and 85%, or the densest 30% window to 85% and 70%. Eighteen variants finished. They fall into three regimes:

RegimeCasesWhat happens
Absorbednewblue2 under every global cut and hotspot 85%; adaptec4 under global cutsZero overflow with 2–5% longer wires, at 2–4× the parent's time
Hotspot residueadaptec2 hotspot 85% (56)Like newblue1/newblue4: one hotspot, oscillating, the region stage working 500 rounds and the portfolio's variants differing
Spread overflowThe other eleven (1,008 to 60,982)The region stage's first round multiplies the overflow 1.8–30×, it often never gets back below its start, the portfolio's variants come back within ~1%, refinement does the work, and runs take 10–73× the parent's time

What belongs to each design is headroom. newblue2 absorbs every global cut down to 85% and tips only under its hotspot at 70%; adaptec4 absorbs global cuts but not its hotspot at 85%; adaptec1, adaptec2 and bigblue3 tip at a 5% global cut. An apparent per-design oscillation trait mostly measured how much overflow was left (Spearman −0.65 against it), with a smaller offset: bigblue3 is the least oscillating at every level. The early stop and the slow-tier stop both came out of the spread-overflow regime.

A pre-routing congestion spectrum (2D Fourier transform of the RUDY map, Part 139) was added to the Difficulty Atlas as a predictor and predicts nothing reliably; its one apparent success rested on one design family.

Process

  • A bug and its safeguard. The slow-tier stop's setting was read in main() after the slow tier had already run, and its self-test set the value directly, so three runs tested nothing (Part 148). An audit found it the only setting read late. gr/e2e_settings.py now runs the router itself with each new setting through the environment and looks for evidence the mechanism acted (15 checks, about a minute, in CI on every push); it fails on the buggy binary. CLAUDE.md: every new setting gets a check there.
  • Settings files are loaded line by line. Sourcing fast08.env with . cuts the portfolio's value at its first ;. The batch script was never affected.
  • Long runs trimmed. Runs on spread-overflow variants reached 10–15 hours for little; the hotspot-70% and 85%-global batches were deferred or cancelled once the regimes were clear.

Still open

  • A wall-aware search heuristic: a lower bound on the remaining cost from a search on a coarse grid priced at each coarse cell's cheapest edge, which would stay exact in cost but stop the flood across the cheap parts of a box. The data point straight at it.
  • "Make room" moves: moving legal nets near a hotspot into spare areas to free tracks for the overflowed ones.
  • The slow tier on spread overflow still runs long; its stop helps but is a trade, not a fix.
  • The channel model from TI experience, once the flow reaches detailed routing, where use inside a GCell decides routability.
