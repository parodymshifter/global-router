Global Router Speed and Theory

The first version of this page asked why the router took 16.9 hours on mempool_cluster_ranking, the largest ISPD 2024 design it had routed, when the contest winners took minutes. That run now takes 461 seconds on 8 threads of the same laptop, with a slightly better score. This page looks at where the time goes now, checks the first version's analysis against what actually happened, asks how the answer could have been found sooner, and maps where there is still room in time, wirelength and overflow.

What it did not see

  • The objective had changed. Every stage was built for ISPD 2008's zero-overflow goal. Nobody re-asked whether each stage still earned its time under the 2024 score. Two of them made the new score worse or no better.
  • Work hidden inside a stage. The pattern stage refreshed costs over the whole grid after each of thousands of batches. That is invisible on small designs and dominant at 10 million nets.
  • The competitor's flow. The winner's source showed one pattern pass, one rip-up, one detour pass and no negotiation loop. That alone said the gap was algorithmic, two orders of magnitude, not a matter of cores.

How to find this sooner

What analysis discipline would have surfaced these findings on day one

  1. Report the objective's change per stage, every run

    The first version had stage times, from a memory log, but not what each stage bought. Time and score change side by side show at once a stage that costs hours and earns nothing. The router now prints that table on every run.

  2. Re-ablate when the objective changes

    When the score function changes, every inherited stage gets switched off one at a time and earns its place again. Here that one experiment was worth 12×.

  3. Check the competitor's runtime and flow first

    A gap of 100× or more is never hardware or constant factors. It means work the other side is not doing, and their flow says which.

  4. Profile at full scale, by operation

    Costs that grow with grid size times batch count only show on the largest design. A sampling profile of one cluster-size run beats any amount of reasoning on small ones.

  5. Measure a parallel scheme's ceiling before trusting it

    For a dependency graph, the ceiling is the number of tasks over the longest chain. One trace showed the slow tier's ceiling was 2.8× before any tuning: hot nets pile onto the same hotspots. That pointed to batching instead.

These five rules are kept in the project's notes and the assistant's persistent memory, so the next algorithm review starts from them rather than rediscovering them.

The theory, revisited

What the model of the routers says now

The first version modeled every router as four choices in one loop: price update, the per-net search (oracle), acceptance, schedule. That model explained where quality came from. Runtime needs a second, simpler model:

time = Σ over stages ( work_s / effective parallelism_s ) work_s = what the objective still needs from stage s (zero for a stage that earns nothing) parallel_s = tasks / longest dependency chain, if regions commute = threads × (1 − conflicts) × balance, if batched against snapshots

Read that way, the steps were:

  • Part 115 set work to zero for stages that earned nothing, and cut redundant work inside the others.
  • Part 116 used the fact that nets touching disjoint regions commute, for parallelism that cannot change the answer.
  • Part 118 handled the one stage where regions overlap: speculate in batches and redo the conflicts.
  • Part 120 moved the pattern stage onto the dependency graph. Its later rounds were thousands of small batches, each waiting for the last, with the threads idle 87% of the time; now each piece waits only for the earlier pieces it overlaps. The 3D passes stopped rebuilding their graph three times.
  • Part 119 changed which nets share a batch. Consecutive nets sit together, so a batch landed on one hotspot and 78% of the search work was thrown away on conflicts. Dealing nets round-robin over tiles cut conflicts from 20,740 to 2,784 and halved the public cluster's slow tier. The project notes said it from the start: the scheduler is not just a work queue, because it decides which nets see the same prices.

The quality model still matters. Its lesson, a restricted search caps quality, is why the router's score beats the winner. The two models just answer different questions.

Part 120 took the public cluster from 658 s to 584 s and the hidden one from 511 s to 461 s, every route unchanged. The big stages are now limited by their own work, not by waiting. Closing the rest of the gap to the winner's 217 s needs less work per piece and per net, or a GPU path for at least one of the three big stages.

On ISPD 2008 the time story has not started. The 2008 standard is still single-threaded at 1 to 27 hours on the large benchmarks. Its time is in the slow tier and the region stage. The 2008 router's batched threading reached 2.4×, but it was not adopted because newblue1's residue grew. Part 119's finding, that unspread batches waste most of their work on conflicts, is a reason to try it again with spreading.

Wirelength

  • ISPD 2024. On the hidden cluster our wire cost is 0.45% above contest #1's (1,191.2M against 1,185.9M), while our via cost is 3.9% and our overflow cost 1.5% below. Two-pin and three-pin nets already route at exactly their bounding box, and multi-pin trees came within 0.2–0.8% of an iterated 1-Steiner reference (Part 105). So better trees for multi-pin nets can recover up to about half a percent of wire. The slow tier's detours are small (+0.08% wire on the hidden cluster, +0.37% on the public one) and the 3D passes take them back. The winner spends less on wire and more on vias, so the wire/via balance in layer assignment is worth one sweep.
  • ISPD 2008. Within −0.05% to +2.3% of NTHU-Route 2.0 on the eleven zero-overflow benchmarks, more where overflow remains (bigblue4 +9%). The 3D pattern pass shortens all sixteen by 0.13–0.45% and is waiting on one confirmation run to join the 2008 standard.

Overflow

  • ISPD 2024. Of the hidden cluster's 309M overflow cost, 234M is what every idle GCell pays under the contest's formula, and no router can avoid it. GCells actually over capacity contribute only 3.3M. The other 71M comes from busy but legal GCells, so the room is in spreading load more evenly across layers and neighbors: Z shapes and wider detours in the 3D pass are the obvious next step. A certified lower bound per design, which v1 proposed and nobody has built yet, would say how much of the 71M is reachable at all.
  • ISPD 2008. Five benchmarks keep a residue: newblue1 38 (another router reached 0), newblue7 108 (at least 54 provably forced; best published 68), newblue4 144 (pin-bound, at 98.4% of its tightest cut), bigblue4 356 (best published 166), newblue3 38,916 (at least 26,264 provably forced; best published about 31,000). bigblue4 and newblue7 have the clearest measured room. The project's record says the next real gain there comes from changing what the search can do, not from another price formula.

Scale

A cluster design now needs about 11 minutes and 23 GB, which fits a larger GitHub runner. tera_cluster, about 5× bigger, needs a 21 GB download and a scaling check first.
