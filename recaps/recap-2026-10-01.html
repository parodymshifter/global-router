Against the 2008 contest routers

Overflow

On par or better on most benchmarks. We reach zero where NTHU-Route 2.0's re-run did not on bigblue2, bigblue3 and newblue5, and leave smaller residues on newblue1 and newblue4. We are behind on three large benchmarks: bigblue4 (370 vs 256, best 166), newblue7 (108 vs 68) and newblue3 (38,704 vs about 31,000, where no published router gets near zero). The one benchmark another router takes to zero and we do not is newblue1 (LMgr 0, ours 48).

Wirelength

Close, with a gap we can now explain. Several benchmarks are within the run-to-run noise. The larger gaps follow detour, wire beyond the pins' minimum spanning tree, not via count. On adaptec1, against NTHU-Route 2.0's own route file, our wire is shorter (3,577,920 vs 3,585,305 tile edges) and the whole gap is vias, from 10% more bends.

Runtime

Still the biggest difference. We now beat NTHU-Route 2.0's time on newblue7 (19.6 h vs 24.9 h) but are 1.5 to 20 times slower on the others, and more than that once the faster machine is accounted for.

Design

The pipeline converged on NTHU-Route 2.0's: present-demand pricing with every reroute kept, 2-pin-piece rip-up with growing search boxes, a refinement stage, NTHU-style layer assignment. What is ours: the hotspot stage that reroutes every net in the box, the tree rebuild and bend-aware wirelength cleanup, a batched pattern stage with bit-identical CPU and GPU (Metal) backends, deterministic work-gated stops, and verification against the official scorer on every result with a measured noise floor.

What the results point to

The piece rip-up stage is the lever for both wirelength and runtime

A per-stage trace shows the same shape on every benchmark measured. The pattern stage ends at the pins' minimum spanning tree. The piece rip-up stage then adds 24 to 34% detour, and the stages after it spend their time recovering 70 to 80% of it. What is left is the wirelength gap. The added detour is concentrated: 1.6 to 3.4% of the nets carry 78 to 90% of it. Nets detoured while congestion was everywhere stay detoured after it clears, because only overflowing pieces are ever rerouted. The same stage takes most of the runtime, so a fix there could improve both.

Stopping on a plateau works when cost is measured correctly

The work-gated stop ended bigblue4's hotspot stage at round 150 instead of 500, with an identical result, cutting runtime by 62%. Measuring work as search-node expansions instead of wall time keeps runs reproducible. The same method of replaying recorded traces first, then building, is ready for the piece rip-up stage.

The overflow residues look structural

All remaining overflow comes from 2D routing; layer assignment adds none. Each residue sits in one hotspot, or a tight group, except newblue7's seven scattered ones. Residues move by only about 2 units between equivalent runs, and tuning has not moved them. They likely need a different kind of mechanism, such as virtual capacity or a separate capacity limit for long nets, not another setting.

Noise sets the rules for judging changes

Three runs that differ only in a harmless setting give overflow within 2 units but wirelength spread 1.6% (newblue1, bigblue2, newblue6) to 3.6% (newblue4). One such run lost bigblue2's zero (it ended at 2). So a single run's wirelength gain below about 2% means nothing, and one lost zero is evidence, not proof.

Still to test, in recommended order

The order is by bounded upside and cost. Runtime ideas can be predicted from recorded traces; quality ideas can only be bounded, not predicted, so each is judged against the benchmark's own noise.

  1. Cut detour in the piece rip-up stage. Two directions: add less detour while congestion is still high, or reroute the 2–3% of heavily detoured nets back toward their tree length as congestion clears. This is the one change that could improve wirelength and runtime together. next
  2. One hotspot box per cluster, for newblue7. Its seven scattered hotspots would touch 5.7 times fewer nets with separate boxes. Mainly a runtime idea, possibly a quality one.
  3. newblue1's last 48: virtual capacity. FastRoute 3.0's mechanism, pricing against a lowered capacity inside the hotspot. The one router-specific idea not yet tried here.
  4. A separate capacity limit for long nets. A second price that pushes long nets out of a contested edge before local nets feel it. Structural and untried; apparently not in any contest router, which makes it the best candidate for this project's novel contribution.
  5. Fewer bends. On adaptec1 the whole wirelength gap is vias from 10% more bends than NTHU-Route 2.0.
  6. Plateau stop for the piece rip-up stage. A newblue3-only runtime gain.
  7. Starting prices from a congestion estimate. Probably low value, since the pattern stage already prices by congestion.

Deferred until a benchmark can score them: timing-driven priorities and a fairness objective (ISPD 2025 / OpenROAD), giant-net splitting (ISPD 2024 scale), provenance tracking (a debugging tool), and the return-to-placement exit (needs placement).

Roadmap steps

  • Step 3, ISPD 2024 format: verifier done and cross-checked; next is the router reading the format (pins as sets of access points, uneven edge lengths, fractional capacities, vias that use capacity, a soft exponential overflow cost).
  • Step 4, multithreading; step 5, GPU maze search and the CUDA CI job; step 6, OpenROAD. See the next two sections.

Where multithreading fits

  • Where the time goes. Almost all runtime is maze searches in the piece rip-up, hotspot and refinement stages. The pattern stage is already batched and runs on the GPU, but it is a small share.
  • How. Group nets whose bounding boxes do not overlap and route each group on all cores at once against one frozen price snapshot. Results stay reproducible run to run. The design notes expect 30–50% parallel efficiency, roughly 3–5 times faster on this machine's performance cores.
  • When. After the piece rip-up detour fix. Both change the same stage, and the project's rule is not to parallelize a part of the router that is still changing. Parallel batching also reorders reroutes, which moves results within the noise, so it needs the noise rules above to judge.
  • Natural fits already found. Separate hotspot boxes (backlog item 2) are disjoint by construction, so they can run in parallel with no extra coordination. The ISPD 2024 contest assumed 8 CPU cores plus GPUs, so that is also the scale to tune thread counts at.

Where OpenROAD fits now

  • It is the evaluator for the next contest format. ISPD 2025 uses the same .cap/.net inputs as 2024, but writes routes in layout coordinates and scores timing and power through a forked OpenROAD. So the 2024 reader is the direct prerequisite.
  • It tests what the 2008 numbers cannot. Global-routing overflow does not show whether a route can actually be detail-routed cleanly. OpenROAD's detailed router and design-rule checks answer that on real LEF/DEF designs.
  • It gives a baseline and timing. Its FastRoute-derived global router is a known-good comparison, and OpenSTA supplies the timing needed for the project's planned net typing (design stage 6).
  • First step, when it comes: build the forked OpenROAD and run its evaluation on one ISPD 2025 case with a trivial solution, the same verify-first approach used for 2008 and 2024. Order: after the 2024 router works and after multithreading, as agreed.
